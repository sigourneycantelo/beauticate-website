Fifteen years ago, James Vivian turned up at my house in Mosman with a little brown doctor's bag, when my son Max was a baby, and pulled out a chemical peel. I remember thinking this guy really knew his stuff. He was just starting out. Today he's one of Australia's most trusted skin clinicians, with [a clinic in Toorak](https://jamesvivian.co), the [Viviology](https://jamesvivian.co/collections/viviology) range and a following who adore him on [Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/jamesvivian/) for being funny, honest and unbothered about looking silly.

This time I went to his. We recorded at the Melbourne home he shares with his husband Ben and their 23-week-old dachshund Wales, who spent most of the interview being nippy, adorable and impossible to ignore.

<QuickAnswer question="What does dermal clinician James Vivian say about healing and confidence?">
James Vivian says the things that hurt him most, bullying, feeling like an outsider and a family that couldn't talk about it, are also what made him empathetic and thorough with clients. He's sober, made peace with his parents through family therapy, and believes confidence comes from healing rather than perfect skin.

It began on the couch, with sausage dogs and renovation plans. It ended somewhere much deeper, and I'm so glad he trusted me with it.

## At home with James, Ben and Wales

The house found them. They knew the people who lived there, and when James and Ben were outgrowing their little home in Richmond near the end of Covid, he texted the owner. "I said, if you ever want to sell your house, let us know," he says. "And he said, well, great timing." It's architectural, beautifully calm, and five minutes from the clinic.

Wales is the other big decision. Ben had always wanted a dog, a big one. James had never owned a dog and was, by his own account, a bit intimidated by them. "I always thought a sausage dog would be great," he says. "And he was like, no, that's pathetic. They're too small." Then Ben met one and fell in love. "And then me being me, I had a sausage dog lined up by the end of that day."

## On the clinic, lockdown and the year it caught up with him

James and Ben work together, which he describes as pros and cons, and the way it started was, in his words, "a moment of desperation". Melbourne's clinics were closed for the better part of two years. James's business grew anyway, through social media and online education, so every time the doors reopened he was looking after his regulars and a swell of new clients he'd only ever met on a screen, before going straight back into lockdown.

"We had grown out our therapists and we had new clients," he says, "but what we hadn't developed was the support structure in the business." Everyone around him was celebrating being free. He wasn't. He hadn't had a drink in years, and he started drinking again.

"And then, you know, all the wheels fell off," he says. "There's a funny photo on the back somewhere at the clinic, and it is me in the fetal position on the floor around Christmas of 2022." Ben could see he was struggling. "In an extraordinary gesture of love, he said, look, I'll look after the clinic for you. You go and sort yourself out."

## On rehab, and getting kicked out of it

James booked in to rehab for thirty days, and he'll tell you the funniest part first. "The title of my memoir would be I Got Kicked Out of Rehab," he says. The place was new, cold, short on salad and long on videos, and when the daily check-ins came round he said out loud what the whole group was feeling. "Because I was the only one speaking out, I got removed. They called me a teaspoon of tar in a jar full of honey."

What stays with him is what could have happened to someone who needed that treatment. "Imagine if I'd been at risk of harming myself, of using again," he says. He didn't need convincing, though. ...

<PullQuote>The minute they picked me up in the car, I actually knew that I would never drink again.</PullQuote>

... "I have amazing willpower when I have it," he says, "but what I had just lost over that period of time was my willpower." Getting in that car and packing his bags was enough. He hasn't had a drink since. "I know how much better my life is. I'm a better husband, father to Wales, boss, therapist."

I've done a stint in rehab myself, so we swapped notes. Mine involved a foam topper and silk pillowcases. His involved no blankets and Ben posting one in.

## On being the odd one out

I asked what kind of little boy he was. "I really grieve for my childhood and for who I was as a kid," James says. "I was pretty unhappy." He was the kid who never felt like he fitted in, who always had the sense that everyone else was in on something he wasn't, and a big part of that was his sexuality. He grew up, as he puts it, in a middle world, when being gay wasn't illegal but wasn't understood either.

He wasn't sporty, either, in a third-generation Wesley College family where his dad had excelled at sport. His way out was to do a deal with his Year 10 PE teacher. "I said, look, I think we all know sport's not for me, but I'm really into anatomy and physiology. Could I be doing some writing and some research?" The teacher told him that in forty years nobody had ever asked. "So I got out of Saturday sport."

## On the bullies, and the night one of them apologised

Boys didn't like that he was friends with the girls, and the girls couldn't be friends with him either. Years later, at a bar when he was about eighteen, he found himself standing next to one of his Year 7 bullies. "The instinct was to run," James says. "But I was like, no, that was so long ago and I'm now much stronger." So he pushed his ID across the bar and introduced himself.

"He grabbed me and took me away from the service area, and he just goes, I've been wanting to apologise to you for years. I can't believe how mean I was to you. It was only because the girls just wanted to hang out with you." James is grateful for the apology, and it's a bit frustrating too. There were a couple of other occasions, and the explanation was always the same. ...

<PullQuote>It wasn't because of who I was or anything negative I was doing, apart from just existing.</PullQuote>

... When he came out at fifteen, he says it was extraordinarily liberating. "The world just made sense all of a sudden. And then I found people that were my people." It's also, he says, where some of the trouble started, because he was out and having an immense amount of fun at an age when nobody else was.

## On his parents, and four sessions of family therapy

Family was harder. James describes parents who gave him a very long leash and who weren't ready to celebrate him when he came out. "My parents are very Darwinian," he says. "We have our children, we give them everything they need, and then it's up to us." That made him resilient and it also left real resentment, and it all came to a head this year, in the lead-up to his mum's 70th birthday and a family trip to Greece.

"We'd had an argument over Christmas, and Mum said something she'd never said before. She said, why don't we get a bit of therapy?" ...

<PullQuote>She said, I just want you to have some peace.</PullQuote>

... The family went into therapy together. James doesn't want to go into it too much, but after three or four sessions the change was extraordinary. He says they "had to go to some really deep dark places," but his parents went from a complete lack of accountability to the opposite, taking full responsibility and understanding the impact it had on him. They wrote each other letters. His mum said the jigsaw puzzle had come together.

Do they have peace now? "Yeah, I do," he says. They went to Greece together and it was so much better. "If we did have a little argument, we were arguing about what we were arguing about. There wasn't all this subtext."

## On empathy, and why he follows up with every client

James calls himself a people pleaser. "For such a long time it felt like I didn't have people appreciating me or wanting to get to know me," he says. "I really like people liking me now. So I live to please." As a dermal clinician, he says, that's useful, because the treatment room is only a small part of the story.

"What happens in the treatment room is a teeny tiny part of the journey," he says. "We do a laser, we put a peel on, we set into motion a chain of events that happen after a client leaves." So he has a strict follow-up policy. Follow up if it's a first visit. Follow up if it's the first time they've had that treatment. Follow up if it's stronger than before. "You can never follow up too much," he says, though not so much that it becomes "James again."

It shapes how he thinks about inclusion too. When he launched [Viviology](https://jamesvivian.co/collections/viviology) with Adore Beauty, he insisted on price points that left room for everyone: a premium vitamin C serum for some and a far more affordable one for others. And on the pressure that sits under every aesthetic treatment, he's blunt. "We have a thing that we say at work: it's not what you did that matters, it's how you fixed it."

## On the sunscreen debate, and hats

James says he felt uncomfortable talking about the sunscreen controversy, because he felt out of his depth and, like a lot of people, as if the rug had been pulled out from under him. What he's landed on is simple. "Any sunscreen is better than no sunscreen," he says. Wear it, use enough, reapply, don't sit in the sun all day, and add a hat.

He told me about a client who was cross about the sunscreen news while admitting she only wore it in summer. "I was like, do you know what? Don't worry about what's going on in the sunscreen land. Just wear your sunscreen." As for the hat: "A hat is always a hat. A sunscreen is not always a sunscreen, because you might not use enough, it's rubbed off, it's washed off." He brought out his hat collection mid-interview, including an umbrella hat, and told me he got married in one.

## On Tina Toner, music and a job on a train

James studied music, and for a long time it was his safe space. "I would go to the music school, find a little room and play piano," he says. Beauty arrived by accident, when a man on a train offered him a part-time sales job at Aesop. His first shift came with a facial from a facialist called Maria. "Whatever you just did to me is what I want to do to people," he told her.

Years later, during lockdown, the two worlds finally met. He'd been running a daily live skincare routine on Instagram and, near the end of one isolation challenge, he put on a wig and announced that "James has gone to mask rehab" and a woman called Tina Toner was stepping in. She lip-synced. She rewrote lyrics. She became the vehicle for his music videos, and eventually he did them as himself. "Doing it at home in the bathroom with no one watching was much easier," he says.

## On ADHD, downtime and what he actually does to switch off

James was diagnosed with ADHD late. His psychiatrist told him he'd harnessed it into a bit of a superpower. What he needs most is downtime, which means the couch, Ben, Wales and a show he's watched many times. "I can't watch a new show or a movie at the moment," he says. It's Disney, The Good Wife, The Nanny and Gossip Girl on repeat.

His wellness is all or nothing. Some stretches he's the epitome of health, eating well, exercising, sleeping. Others, none of it. He takes a fistful of supplements too, and is honest that he's a skeptic about all of it, himself included. "I take it with a hope," he says.

## Behind the mic: what you'll learn from this chat

**Why did James Vivian go to rehab?** He says the stress of coming out of Covid lockdowns, with a business that had grown faster than its support structure, led him back to drinking. He booked in for thirty days, was asked to leave after speaking up for the group, and hasn't had a drink since.

**How did family therapy change things for James?** It took three or four sessions. He says his parents moved from a lack of accountability to taking full responsibility, and the family trip to Greece they'd feared became a happy one.

**What does James Vivian think about the sunscreen controversy?** That nobody has all the answers yet, that any sunscreen beats none, and that you should keep wearing it, use enough and add a hat.

**Why does James follow up with every client?** Because he says the treatment room is only a small part of the journey, and what happens afterwards is where he can tell whether he's done a good job.

## James's Sacred Six

We finish every conversation with the Sacred Six: the six things that make our guests look and feel their best. James's:

1. **An Aesop bottle.** Where his beauty career began, in a part-time sales job more than twenty years ago.
2. **A foaming cleanser, and the ritual.** Controversial, he says, but more people should use one: give it a full 60 seconds, work it into areas of roughness, use a headband to get your hair off your face, and cleanse all the way down to your bust.
3. **Aesop's Marrakech.** He was there when Aesop launched its first perfume. It's a special-occasion scent, with spice and nothing too floral.
4. **A Virtue hair mask.** Nourishing, and it doesn't look greasy. "Worlds apart" from the others he's tried.
5. **A Restore mask by Cosmedix.** It's lightly exfoliating, he wears it around the house, and he's not above driving with it on.
6. **Sunscreen.** Every day, and reapplied. "I wear sunscreen every day. It's not a secret."
